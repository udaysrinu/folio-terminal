Why a basket travels as percentages, and never as an order list

Two people follow the same basket with different amounts of money. When it rebalances, what exactly does one send the other? Getting this wrong doesn't produce a small error — it produces somebody else's portfolio. This page is the reasoning, with the arithmetic worked out.

01The thing that is actually being shared

A basket can be written down two ways.

As share countsAs target weights
RELIANCE 40
TCS 27
HDFCBANK 58
NTPC 228		 RELIANCE 35.0%
TCS 20.0%
HDFCBANK 25.0%
NTPC 20.0%
Only true for one person, at one amount of money, on one day. True for everyone, at any amount, on any day.

Share counts are a result. Weights are the instruction. The moment you send share counts you have sent an answer computed for your wallet, and the recipient has a different wallet.

So a sheet holds weights and reference prices. Your quantities are derived from it, locally, from your own capital. Nobody's share counts ever travel.

02Investing picks an amount. Rebalancing cannot.

These look like the same operation and they are not. They are duals — what is free in one is determined in the other.

First-time buyRebalance
You supplyan amountyour existing holdings
Comes outshare countsthe difference from target
Free to chooseamountnothing
 
first-time buy      qty    = round(amount × weight / price)

rebalance           value  = Σ (shares you hold × price)      ← not a choice
                    target = round(value × weight / price)
                    order  = target − shares you hold

This is why the workbench offers you multiples of the minimum when you buy in, and offers you no multiplier at all when you rebalance. There is nothing to multiply: your capital is already whatever your holding is worth.

"I bought 2× the minimum, my friend bought 1×. The rebalance is 2× of my holdings. How does his app know to use 1×?"

It never needs to know. He isn't given a multiple of anything — he's given weights, and he applies them to his own value. The multiplier is not transmitted because it is not a property of the basket. It's a property of a wallet, and each wallet supplies its own.

03What happens if you send the order list instead

Worth seeing, because it is the intuitive thing to do and it fails hard. Real numbers: a four-stock basket with a ₹14,809 floor, one holder at ₹4,00,000 (27×) and one at ₹80,000 (5.4×). The manager moves RELIANCE 30% → 35% and TCS 25% → 20%.

The 27× holder's own orders

RELIANCE  +7      TCS  −5      HDFCBANK  ±0      NTPC  −2

Sent as weights — correct

The 5.4× holder's app sizes those weights to his ₹78,956 and produces RELIANCE +1, TCS −1, HDFCBANK −1, NTPC −1. He ends up 0.78 percentage points from the manager's target.

Sent as that order list — broken

He holds 5 TCS. The instruction says sell 5. His entire TCS position is wiped out and RELIANCE inflates to 55.3% against a 35% target. He lands 20.26 percentage points off.

Sent asWorst deviation from target
Weights0.78 pp
Share-count orders20.26 pp

The failure is not proportional. A sell sized for a big holding doesn't shrink gracefully against a small one — it goes through zero, and a position vanishes.

04Whole shares are the real enemy

Exchanges won't sell you 0.4 of a share, so every basket is an approximation. The size of the approximation is set by the priciest stock relative to your capital, and it is why a basket has a floor at all:

scale   = max over stocks of ( price / target weight )
minimum = round up ( sum over stocks of ( price × round down ( scale × weight / price ) ) )

Below that, the priciest stock can't afford a single share. Most tools quietly drop it and hand you a more concentrated portfolio than you asked for. This one refuses, and says which stock set the floor.

The two lines matter. We shipped only the first one at the start, and it was wrong — it overstated this basket's floor by ₹12,714 and refused amounts that buy a perfectly complete basket. scale is the point where the binding stock reaches exactly one share, but every other stock rounds down from its fractional target at that point, so the cash you actually need is strictly less. The gap grows with the number of holdings and the spread of their weights.

The corrected version reproduces a minimum investment amount smallcase publishes for one of its own baskets — ₹385, from a true cost of ₹384.7156 — to the rupee. That is how we know it's right rather than merely plausible, and it's the regression test in selfTest().

The last step rounds up, and that direction is not arbitrary: a minimum that rounded down would be short of its own basket. Publish ₹384 for a basket that costs ₹384.72 and someone who invests exactly the stated minimum cannot buy it — the same defect as overstating, just a rupee wide instead of ₹12,714. So the inner step rounds down (you cannot buy part of a share) and the outer step rounds up (you must cover the whole bill).

Honest caveat: smallcase documents the definition of its minimum but publishes no formula and no rounding rule. Rounding up is the only self-consistent reading, not a confirmed fact.

How coarse is a basket, exactly?

One share is the smallest move you can make, so the priciest stock's share of the total is the resolution of the whole basket:

resolution (pp per share) = highest price / total value × 100
Your holdingOne share of TCS moves the basketVerdict
₹29,127 (2×)12.71 ppunusable as a shared sheet
₹1,48,090 (10×)2.50 ppborderline
₹3,98,055 (27×)0.93 ppfine to share

At 2× the minimum a position is literally 3, 2, 4, 17 shares. A five point change in target weight produced zero orders — the rounding was larger than the rebalance. Small holdings can't express fine instructions.

05Which means the biggest holder should publish

A sheet reconstructed from real holdings inherits that holder's rounding. Its weights are not the manager's exact intent — they're the closest thing expressible at that wallet size.

Publisher's holdingError baked into the sheet they share
2×5.42 pp
5×0.54 pp
27×0.36 pp
200×0.04 pp

Everyone who sizes that sheet inherits the publisher's error and then adds their own. So the rule is counter-intuitive but firm: whoever holds the most should be the one who shares the sheet. The workbench states a sheet's resolution when it builds one, and says plainly when it is too coarse to pass on.

Is there a fractional multiplier?

Not on the capital, because that would only restate the cash box. "Rebalance at 0.5×" means "withdraw half my value", and rupees are the better unit: you know you have ₹50,000 to add, not that you want 1.15× of your portfolio. It is also ambiguous — half of your current value, or half of the target?

There is a useful fraction, and it scales the orders rather than the capital:

order = round( (target_qty − held) × k )        k = 1.00, 0.75, 0.50, 0.25

Moving part of the way cuts turnover and defers realising gains, at the cost of staying deliberately off target. One exception is hard-coded: a stock the basket has dropped is always sold in full regardless of k. Part-exiting leaves a stub in your account forever, which is worse than either finishing or not starting.

Unsettled shares count as held

Buy today and the broker reports quantity: 0, t1_quantity: 9 until settlement. Read only the quantity column and a just-executed rebalance looks like it never happened, so the next run tells you to buy the whole position again. Both holdings parsers add T1 to the settled quantity.

06State, not events

A rebalance email is an event — "I bought these, I sold those." A sheet is state — "the basket is now this." Events only make sense against the exact state they were computed from, which is why forwarding one to a friend cannot work: his prior state is different.

So the flow is: you apply the event to your state, which yields new state, and the new state is what you pass on.

manager's rebalance email
   ↓ applied to your holdings
your updated sheet  (weights)
   ↓ sent to whoever follows you
their own orders, sized to their own money

What if someone forwards you the email anyway?

It is the obvious thing to do, so the workbench checks for it. A rebalance email's quantities were computed against one specific position, and there are two detectable signatures when it meets a different one:

  • It asks to sell more than the sheet holds. Arithmetically impossible — refused outright, with the numbers shown.
  • It sells a meaningful holding down to exactly zero, or moves more than about a third of the basket's value. Both are unusual for a real rebalance and strongly suggest the email belongs to a larger position. Flagged, with the advice to ask for the sender's sheet.

Without those checks the result is not an error message — it is a plausible-looking basket with a stock missing. A SELL 5 sized for a 27× holding exactly cancels a 5× holder's entire position, so the stock silently disappears and the sheet claims one fewer holding than the manager published.

One thing the sheet cannot fix. A stock held through two different baskets appears once in your broker's holdings, as one merged quantity. Any per-basket drift calculated from that number is wrong for both baskets. The workbench flags it rather than pretending the number means something.

07What this buys you

  • One file works at ₹20,000 and at ₹20,00,000, with no versions and no per-person maths.
  • Nobody learns anybody else's position size. Weights reveal proportions, never amounts.
  • Rebalances compose: apply five in a row and you still have valid state, because each step produces state rather than a diff to be replayed.
  • It works with no server, because all of it is arithmetic on two small numbers per stock.
